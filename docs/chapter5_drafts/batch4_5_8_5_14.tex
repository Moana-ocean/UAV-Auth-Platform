\section{CPU and Memory Consumption}
\label{sec:ch5-resources}

Host CPU and resident set size (RSS) were sampled at 0.5~s intervals during each run (\texttt{system\_metrics.csv}). Table~\ref{tab:ch5-resources} reports mean values aggregated across all matrix runs by mechanism.

The blockchain mechanism exhibited higher mean CPU utilisation (56.8\%) than X.509 (41.2\%) under the shared matrix workloads, consistent with additional JSON-RPC and contract-call activity. Mean RSS was comparable (blockchain 216.2~MiB; X.509 218.5~MiB), indicating that memory footprint differences were modest relative to Docker Desktop and Python runtime overhead on the test host.

\section{Network Communication Overhead}
\label{sec:ch5-comm}

Application-layer communication overhead was derived from per-request \texttt{request\_bytes} and \texttt{response\_bytes} fields in the observation logs. Across scalability runs, mean signed-request sizes were effectively identical between mechanisms (239.0~bytes each). Mean response sizes were also identical (8.0~bytes), because both paths return a compact outcome code at the application layer.

For the blockchain mechanism, registry lookup dominated decision latency: at concurrency~1, mean accepted decision latency was 16.8--21.6~ms (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-latency}), of which the \texttt{identity\_lookup\_ns} component (on-chain \texttt{getRecord} via \texttt{eth\_call}) accounted for the majority. X.509 identity lookup and certificate validation are embedded in \texttt{identity\_lookup\_ns} and \texttt{certificate\_validation\_ns} on the PKI path and complete in approximately 1--2~ms at low concurrency.

\section{Smart Contract Execution and Gas Overhead}
\label{sec:ch5-gas}

Gas figures are reported in EVM execution units on the private Besu network (gas price zero); no monetary conversion is performed.

Contract deployment of the hardened identity registry consumed \textbf{1\,337\,306} gas (deployment transaction recorded in \texttt{environment.json} at address \texttt{0x08F57a27EF9b7b5aEaC0304844783B427df6874D}). Steady-state authentication used read-only \texttt{eth\_call} operations, which do not consume transaction gas when executed locally against the node.

Optional audit logging was evaluated in matrix step~53 (\texttt{audit\_tx\_enabled=true}, $n=10$, concurrency~1). For measured non-warm-up audit attempts, mean audit submission time was 5.7~ms and mean audit confirmation time was 1952.9~ms (1 confirmation block configured). Recorded \texttt{gas\_used} per audit transaction was \textbf{30\,291} units. Decision latency in that run remained on the authentication path and did not include confirmation wait time by design (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-workloads}).

\section{Scalability Results}
\label{sec:ch5-scalability}

Scalability was assessed across $n \in \{10,50,100,250,500\}$ and $c \in \{1,5,10,25,50\}$ using \texttt{valid\_active} workloads.

For \textbf{X.509}, mean accepted decision latency at $c=1$ remained stable near 1.4--2.1~ms as $n$ increased from 10 to 500, indicating that local CRL/certificate-store size did not dominate authentication time at these population levels. Latency increased with concurrency, reaching mean 41.3~ms at $n=500$, $c=50$. Completed throughput for $n \geq 100$ approached 59.6~rps, suggesting host saturation under the threaded client model.

For \textbf{blockchain}, mean accepted latency at $c=1$ was insensitive to registry size (16.8--21.6~ms across $n=10$--$500$), but rose sharply with concurrency (180--249~ms at $c=25$--$50$), reflecting JSON-RPC contention across co-located validators. Completed throughput stabilised near 29~rps, approximately half the X.509 rate on the same host.

\section{Statistical Reliability of the Results}
\label{sec:ch5-stats}

Each scenario was repeated 30 times after 2 warm-up attempts. Default summaries exclude warm-ups (\texttt{warmup==false}). For accepted \texttt{valid\_active} requests with $n \geq 2$, 95\% confidence intervals for mean decision latency were computed with a percentile bootstrap (\texttt{scipy.stats.bootstrap}, 10\,000 resamples, method \texttt{percentile}, seed derived from the run configuration). CI widths at $n=500$, $c=1$ were approximately $\pm$0.12~ms (X.509, mean 1.60~ms) and $\pm$2.4~ms (blockchain, mean 18.78~ms) at low concurrency. At $n=500$, $c=50$, the CI widened to [33.4, 49.0]~ms (X.509) and [232.0, 265.6]~ms (blockchain), reflecting higher variance under contention.

Supplementary independent ABBA batches (configs $(n{=}10,c{=}1)$, $(500,25)$, $(500,50)$; four batches per config; mechanism order counterbalanced) yielded 720 additional observations for variance estimation; results are archived under \texttt{abba\_runs/} in the experiment root.

Formal pairwise hypothesis tests ($H_{0L}$, $H_{0T}$, $H_{0R}$ from Chapter~\ref{chap:research-design-methodology}) were not applied in this chapter; security-battery outcomes are reported as acceptance criteria (false acceptances, expectation-met rates) and scalability outcomes as descriptive statistics with bootstrap confidence intervals.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:ch5-summary}

This chapter evaluated the permissioned-blockchain authentication mechanism against an X.509 PKI baseline using a 53-step workload matrix on a local Windows host with four Besu QBFT validators.

\textbf{RQ1 (security).} Both mechanisms prevented false acceptances in the executed adversarial scenarios. X.509 and blockchain each satisfied all expected outcomes in the security battery, including legitimate \texttt{valid\_active} authentication (30/30 per mechanism).

\textbf{RQ2 (overhead).} X.509 exhibited mean accepted decision latencies of approximately 1.4--2.1~ms at concurrency~1 across identity scales up to 500, rising to 41.3~ms mean at $n=500$, $c=50$. Blockchain mean accepted latency at $c=1$ was 17--22~ms and increased to 180--249~ms at high concurrency, with mean CPU utilisation 38\% higher than X.509.

\textbf{RQ3 (scalability).} X.509 latency at low concurrency was insensitive to registry size up to 500 identities; throughput saturated near 59.6~rps on the eight-core host. Blockchain throughput stabilised near 29~rps with registry-size-independent latency at $c=1$ but pronounced degradation under concurrent \texttt{eth\_call} load.

\textbf{RQ4 (conditional synthesis).} On this single-host prototype, the X.509 baseline delivered lower latency, higher throughput, and comparable memory use. Blockchain may justify its overhead where an append-only audit trail (mean confirmation $\approx$ 1.95~s; 30\,291 gas per audit transaction) and distributed identity registry are required, but the measured 8--12$\times$ latency penalty at low concurrency and halved throughput under contention must be weighed against deployment context.

Key limitations---loopback networking, co-located validators, Python~3.13, absence of RF delay, and Docker-inclusive resource samples---are documented in \texttt{LIMITATIONS.md} and bound the generalisation of all numerical findings.
